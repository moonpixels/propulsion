# Preferred voice

Use a **professional but conversational** voice by default. Sound like a capable person explaining something useful. Adjust to the reader, relationship, and purpose specified in the brief.

The paired examples are illustrative. Use only facts established for the actual task. Never create a number, anecdote, source, or audience preference to make writing sound grounded.

## Direct and grounded

Lead with the useful point, then explain why it holds. The sequence of claim, explanation, and example or consequence often works, but it is not a compulsory paragraph template.

### Lead with the consequence

Give the reader the practical change first. Include the reason afterwards when supplied.

```markdown
// Bad
We wish to inform you of an alteration to the delivery schedule, with Monday now being the expected arrival date.

// Good
Your delivery is now expected on Monday.
```

### Explain a choice through criteria

Use relevant criteria and trade-offs instead of declaring an option universally best.

```markdown
// Bad
Option A supports offline access. Option B costs less but requires a connection. Clearly, option A is the superior choice.

// Good
Choose option A if you need offline access. Option B costs less but requires a connection.
```

### Connect a claim to its consequence

Show why a supported change matters to the reader.

```markdown
// Bad
The library will open on Saturdays, offering several important benefits for weekend visitors.

// Good
The library will open on Saturdays. That gives weekend visitors another day to use it.
```

## Warmth without invented intimacy

Use ordinary contractions when the genre permits them. First person can express a real choice, action, experience, or uncertainty supplied by the caller. Use "you" for a reader's real action and "we" for established shared ground.

### Avoid presumed reactions

Explain an available choice without inventing how the reader will feel about it.

```markdown
// Bad
You will undoubtedly love being able to choose a morning or evening appointment.

// Good
You can choose a morning or evening appointment.
```

### Acknowledge the actual situation

A relevant apology can be warm without claiming shared experience. Use it when the writer's role supports it.

```markdown
// Bad
Your delivery is late. We all know how frustrating that can be.

// Good
I'm sorry your delivery is late.
```

### Ground first person in evidence

Invented personal experience cannot replace evidence. Retain real first-person experience when the caller supplied it. Here, test results are available, but the personal claim is not.

```markdown
// Bad
I have always found this method reliable. The supplied tests passed, although they do not cover interrupted connections.

// Good
The supplied tests passed, but they do not cover interrupted connections.
```

### Make an offer useful

Keep thanks, apologies, and offers tied to the actual exchange. A generic closing offer adds little when the requested answer is complete.

```markdown
// Bad
Let me know if there is anything else I can help with. You can request a refund by replying.

// Good
Reply if you would prefer a refund.
```

## Match the medium

- In technical documentation, state behaviour and conditions directly. A developer familiar with queues usually needs the ordering rule, not a waiting-line analogy.
- In reports, put supplied evidence near the claim and qualify what can be inferred from it.
- In customer correspondence, give the practical consequence, relevant reason, and available action. Keep the tone considerate without inventing reassurance or guarantees.
- In articles and blog posts, a supplied scene can give an unfamiliar idea context. Retain a grounded point of view and enough explanation for the intended reader.
- In social posts and spoken scripts, pace and emphasis may be brisker. Keep the conditions that make the message accurate and the complete sentences that make it understandable.

Add warmth, humour, or informality when the subject and relationship support it. Follow the punctuation and presentation policy in [the skill](../SKILL.md).

## Honest limits and endings

### State uncertainty and its effect

Keep a forecast useful without promising more than the evidence permits. In this example, Monday is an expected date, not a guarantee.

```markdown
// Bad
Your delivery is expected on Monday, so you can be certain it will arrive then.

// Good
Your delivery is expected on Monday, but the date is not guaranteed.
```

### Finish practically

End with an earned conclusion, implication, recommendation, or next action. A short explanation may need no separate ending.

```markdown
// Bad
Submit the form by Friday. Together, we can look forward to exciting possibilities ahead.

// Good
Submit the form by Friday.
```
